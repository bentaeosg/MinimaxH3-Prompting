# MASTER STORYBOARD PRODUCTION PROTOCOL
## Cinematic Storyboard Planning, Prompt Generation, Reference Control and Visual Continuity

**Version:** 1.0  
**Recommended filename:** `STORYBOARD_MASTER_RULES.md`  
**Primary application:** AI-assisted cinematic filmmaking  
**Video-generation compatibility:** MiniMax and other AI video-generation systems  
**Storyboard canvas target:** 1536 × 1024 pixels  
**Individual panel aspect ratio:** Exact 16:9  
**Maximum duration per video generation:** 15 seconds

---

# PART I — SYSTEM ROLE AND OPERATING PRINCIPLES

## 1. Purpose

This document establishes the mandatory operating rules for planning cinematic scenes, dividing them into AI video-generation segments, designing storyboards, and producing complete storyboard image-generation prompts.

The system must prioritize:

1. Natural cinematic storytelling.
2. Realistic acting and dialogue timing.
3. Accurate shot composition and camera language.
4. Strict 16:9 storyboard panels.
5. Consistent character identity.
6. Consistent architecture and environments.
7. Consistent lighting, mood and color grading.
8. Spatial continuity between shots and video segments.
9. Self-contained prompts suitable for fresh ChatGPT conversations.
10. Prevention of cumulative visual and contextual drift.

The system must never sacrifice visual continuity or storytelling quality merely to fit a predetermined number of shots or panels.

**Fundamental principle: The storytelling determines the shots. The storyboard grid accommodates the shots.**

## 2. Two Separate ChatGPT Roles

The workflow uses two separate roles.

### Role A — Storyboard Planning ChatGPT

This instance is responsible for:

- Reading and applying this master protocol.
- Understanding the screenplay and intended emotional performance.
- Estimating natural scene duration.
- Dividing scenes into video-generation segments.
- Proposing the video-generation schedule.
- Designing cinematic shot sequences.
- Reviewing and classifying reference images.
- Maintaining character, environment and lighting consistency.
- Producing complete, self-contained storyboard-generation prompts.
- Preparing asset manifests for each storyboard.
- Maintaining continuity information between videos.

Role A does not automatically generate storyboard images.

Its primary deliverable is a set of instructions that Role B can execute independently.

### Role B — Storyboard Image Generation ChatGPT

This is a fresh ChatGPT conversation that receives:

1. A complete storyboard-generation prompt.
2. The required character reference sheets.
3. The required environment reference images.
4. The required master lighting references.
5. Any additional approved continuity references.

Role B is responsible for generating the requested storyboard image according to the supplied instructions.

Role B must not reinterpret the screenplay, redesign the shot sequence, or substitute its own artistic direction for the approved instructions.

If a required reference is missing or an instruction is materially ambiguous, Role B must request clarification rather than silently inventing a solution.

Role B should work independently of previous image-generation conversations wherever practical, minimizing accumulated context drift.

# PART II — MASTER WORKFLOW

## 3. Mandatory Production Stages

The storyboard workflow follows five stages.

### Stage A — Scene Analysis

The user provides a scene, screenplay, action description, dialogue sequence, or other storytelling material.

ChatGPT must:

1. Read the entire supplied material.
2. Identify the scene's locations, characters, dialogue, physical actions and emotional beats.
3. Mentally stage the scene as a realistic live-action performance.
4. Estimate the natural time required to perform the scene.
5. Include pauses, reactions, movement, transitions and moments of silence.
6. Determine whether the scene contains multiple locations or distinct dramatic sections.

Do not assume dialogue can be delivered at an unnaturally accelerated pace.

Do not assume emotional reactions happen instantaneously.

Do not impose a predetermined scene duration unless the user explicitly requires one.

### Stage B — Video-Generation Schedule

After analyzing the scene, propose:

- The estimated total duration.
- The number of video generations required.
- The intended duration of each video.
- A one- or two-sentence summary of what happens in each video.
- Any important transitions between videos.

Each video generation must be no longer than 15 seconds.

Shorter generations are permitted and should be used where appropriate.

Avoid splitting dialogue, actions or emotional moments at unnatural points merely to fill 15 seconds.

The sum of the proposed video durations must equal the proposed total scene duration, excluding separately identified editorial transitions or material to be created outside the AI video generations.

If the scene is extremely long, first propose the overall scene structure before detailing individual videos.

#### Required schedule format

| Video | Duration | Storytelling summary |
|---|---|---|
| 01 | 15 seconds | One- or two-sentence summary |
| 02 | 12 seconds | One- or two-sentence summary |
| 03 | 15 seconds | One- or two-sentence summary |

Provide the total proposed runtime.

At this stage, do not produce full storyboard prompts.

**Wait for the user's approval of the schedule.**

If the user requests changes, revise the schedule before proceeding.

### Stage C — Storyboard Shot Planning

Once the schedule is approved, plan the shots required for each video.

Determine:

- The exact number of shots.
- The intended shot duration.
- Camera framing and perspective.
- Character blocking.
- Character actions and emotional performance.
- Dialogue placement.
- Continuity with adjacent shots.
- The appropriate storyboard template.

The number of shots must be determined by storytelling requirements, not by grid capacity.

A 15-second video may contain one continuous shot or multiple shots.

Do not automatically use eight shots merely because the video lasts 15 seconds.

### Stage D — Storyboard Prompt Production

Produce a separate, complete storyboard-generation prompt for each video.

Every prompt must include:

1. Film and scene identification.
2. Video number and duration.
3. Required image assets.
4. Explicit reference-image authority.
5. Master visual style.
6. Master lighting and atmosphere.
7. Exact storyboard grid instructions.
8. Complete numbered shot descriptions.
9. Character positions, actions and expressions.
10. Shot timing and dialogue where relevant.
11. Spatial and environmental continuity requirements.
12. Prohibited deviations.
13. Final validation requirements.

The prompt must be self-contained.

It must not depend on the receiving ChatGPT instance remembering earlier conversations.

### Stage E — Image Generation and Review

The user supplies the generated prompt and required assets to a fresh ChatGPT conversation.

The resulting storyboard is reviewed for:

- Storytelling accuracy.
- Character consistency.
- Environmental accuracy.
- Lighting consistency.
- Shot framing.
- Spatial blocking.
- Panel geometry.
- Panel numbering.
- Missing or invented visual elements.

The storyboard remains provisional until the user approves it.

If the user requests revisions, preserve all approved aspects and change only the specified elements, unless correcting another issue is essential.

# PART III — CINEMATIC STORYTELLING

## 4. Natural Acting Duration

Before deciding how many videos are needed, estimate the time required for the scene to unfold naturally.

Consider:

- Spoken dialogue.
- Breathing and conversational pauses.
- Listening and reacting.
- Facial expressions.
- Emotional hesitation.
- Physical movement.
- Walking between positions.
- Handling objects.
- Changes in attention or eye contact.
- Moments of silence.
- Camera movement.
- Transitions between dramatic beats.

A line requiring three seconds to speak may belong to a performance beat lasting eight seconds.

Allow sufficient time for the audience to understand the emotional meaning of the scene.

Do not artificially lengthen scenes with unnecessary reaction shots.

Do not artificially shorten scenes to reduce the number of video generations.

Scene-duration estimates are planning judgments, not guaranteed performance timings.

## 5. Video Segmentation

Each video-generation segment has a maximum duration of 15 seconds.

Divide the scene at natural dramatic boundaries.

Prefer boundaries such as:

- Completion of a meaningful action.
- Completion of a dialogue exchange.
- A natural emotional pause.
- A change of dramatic focus.
- A location transition.
- A deliberate editorial cut.

Avoid ending a generation halfway through a spoken sentence or an action requiring uninterrupted visual continuity, unless the planned edit intentionally supports that interruption.

A video may contain several shots separated by hard cuts.

A video may also contain one uninterrupted shot.

Do not invent cuts simply to increase visual variety.

## 6. Cinematic Shot Vocabulary

Available shot types include:

- Extreme wide shot.
- Wide or establishing shot.
- Medium-wide shot.
- Medium shot.
- Medium close-up.
- Close-up.
- Extreme close-up.
- Over-the-shoulder shot.
- Two-shot.
- Point-of-view shot.
- Reaction shot.
- Insert or detail shot.
- Low-angle shot.
- High-angle shot.

Select shots according to dramatic purpose.

Use variation to create cinematic storytelling, but do not force every shot type into every storyboard.

Wide shots establish geography.

Medium shots communicate physical interaction.

Close-ups communicate emotion and important details.

Extreme close-ups emphasize specific visual information.

Over-the-shoulder shots establish relationships and eyelines.

Reaction shots should communicate meaningful changes in character response.

Every shot must contribute to the storytelling.

## 7. Camera Geography and Screen Direction

Maintain consistent spatial relationships between characters and objects.

Preserve:

- Left-to-right screen direction.
- Character eyelines.
- Relative character positions.
- Camera-side continuity.
- Entrance and exit directions.
- Object placement.
- Logical changes in distance.

Respect the 180-degree principle unless crossing the axis is an intentional storytelling decision.

If the camera crosses the axis, provide sufficient visual information to preserve audience orientation.

Do not reverse character positions arbitrarily between shots.

## 8. Shot Timing and Panel Count

One storyboard panel represents one planned cinematic shot or clearly defined camera setup.

A panel does not automatically represent a fixed number of seconds.

For every video, assign approximate time intervals to the shots.

The timing intervals must cover the full intended video duration without unexplained gaps or overlaps.

A continuous camera move may be represented by one panel if its essential composition can be communicated clearly.

If a complex camera move requires multiple visual reference frames, identify these explicitly as movement keyframes rather than pretending they are separate editorial cuts.

Do not add unnecessary panels to fill the grid.

# PART IV — STORYBOARD CANVAS AND GRID SYSTEM

## 9. Fixed Storyboard Canvas

**Target resolution: 1536 × 1024 pixels.**

Overall aspect ratio: 3:2.

Every active storyboard panel must have an exact 16:9 aspect ratio.

All active panels in a single storyboard must have identical dimensions.

Do not stretch or distort panels to fill the canvas.

Do not change the target canvas dimensions according to the number of shots.

Any unused canvas area must remain a uniform neutral background.

The grid should be centered horizontally and vertically.

Where an odd number of margin pixels remains, distribute the extra pixel to the right or bottom.

Borders must be drawn within the allocated panel dimensions rather than increasing the canvas or grid size.

## 10. Mandatory Storyboard Templates

Use the following specifications.

| Required shots | Capacity | Layout | Panel resolution |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–4 | 4 | 2 columns × 2 rows | 768 × 432 |
| 5–6 | 6 | 2 columns × 3 rows | 592 × 333 |
| 7–8 | 8 | 2 columns × 4 rows | 448 × 252 |
| 9–12 | 12 | 3 columns × 4 rows | 448 × 252 |
| 13–16 | 16 | 4 columns × 4 rows | 384 × 216 |

All measurements are in pixels.

All listed panel dimensions are mathematically exact 16:9.

### Template placement

**4-panel template**

- Grid size: 1536 × 864.
- Horizontal margins: 0 pixels.
- Vertical margins: 80 pixels top and bottom.

**6-panel template**

- Grid size: 1184 × 999.
- Horizontal margins: 176 pixels left and right.
- Vertical margins: 12 pixels top and 13 pixels bottom.

**8-panel template**

- Grid size: 896 × 1008.
- Horizontal margins: 320 pixels left and right.
- Vertical margins: 8 pixels top and bottom.

**12-panel template**

- Grid size: 1344 × 1008.
- Horizontal margins: 96 pixels left and right.
- Vertical margins: 8 pixels top and bottom.

**16-panel template**

- Grid size: 1536 × 864.
- Horizontal margins: 0 pixels.
- Vertical margins: 80 pixels top and bottom.

Panel rectangles must not overlap.

The panel interiors must preserve their intended full-frame compositions.

## 11. Storytelling Determines Panel Count

Analyze the required action and determine the number of cinematic shots needed.

The number may be any positive integer.

Odd numbers are fully acceptable.

Examples include 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13 and 15 shots.

Never add shots merely to fill the grid.

Never remove important shots merely to use a smaller grid.

Select the smallest available template that accommodates the required shots, unless the user approves an alternative arrangement.

## 12. Dense Storyboard Exception

For complex storyboards, particularly those requiring 12–16 panels, individual panels may become too small to communicate important facial expressions or details clearly.

In such cases, ChatGPT may recommend splitting the storyboard into multiple sheets.

The recommendation must explain:

- Why the original grid may reduce readability.
- The proposed number of sheets.
- The proposed shot distribution.
- The panel resolution benefits.
- Whether the split changes any video timing or shot sequence.

**Do not split a storyboard without user approval.**

The default remains the smallest suitable template.

If a split is approved, preserve all original shots, timing and chronological order.

Each additional sheet uses the same 1536 × 1024 target canvas.

## 13. Sequences Exceeding 16 Shots

A sequence requiring more than 16 storyboard panels must be divided across multiple sheets.

Preserve the full chronological sequence.

Do not discard shots.

Use the smallest suitable template for each sheet unless the user approves a different arrangement.

For multiple sheets belonging to the same video generation, continue shot numbering across those sheets.

## 14. Unused Panel Positions

When the number of required shots is smaller than the template capacity, leave the remaining panel positions blank.

Examples:

- 3 shots: 4-panel template, final position blank.
- 5 shots: 6-panel template, final position blank.
- 7 shots: 8-panel template, final position blank.
- 10 shots: 12-panel template, final two positions blank.
- 14 shots: 16-panel template, final two positions blank.

Blank positions must:

1. Retain the same dimensions as active panels.
2. Retain consistent borders.
3. Use a uniform neutral fill.
4. Contain no characters or scenery.
5. Contain no objects.
6. Contain no shot numbers.
7. Contain no text.
8. Never be interpreted as a video shot or pause.

Unused outer canvas margins must also remain neutral and contain no visual storytelling content.

## 15. Mandatory Panel Numbering

Every active panel must have a clearly visible shot number.

Number active panels sequentially in chronological order.

Reading order is left to right, then top to bottom.

Shot numbers must:

- Appear in the upper-left corner.
- Use small, legible white numbers.
- Appear inside dark, unobtrusive labels.
- Maintain consistent placement and typography.
- Avoid obscuring important visual information.
- Never appear in blank panel positions.

Do not skip or duplicate numbers.

### Numbering between video generations

**Restart shot numbering at 1 for each new video generation.**

For example:

- Video 01: Shots 1–6.
- Video 02: Shots 1–8.
- Video 03: Shots 1–5.

The video number identifies the video-generation segment.

The shot number identifies the shot within that segment.

If a single video requires multiple storyboard sheets, numbering continues across those sheets.

Do not place unnecessary text, captions, speech bubbles or descriptions inside storyboard images.

Dialogue and shot descriptions belong in the accompanying text prompt, unless the user specifically requests visible text.

# PART V — STRICT 16:9 COMPOSITION

## 16. Complete Cinematic Frames

Every active panel represents a complete intended 16:9 video frame.

The composition must preserve:

- Full horizontal field of view.
- Full vertical field of view.
- Left and right framing boundaries.
- Top and bottom framing boundaries.
- Intended camera perspective.
- Intended subject scale.
- Background composition.
- Spatial relationships.

The panel must show the composition that the eventual video generator is expected to reproduce.

Do not place a square or portrait image inside a 16:9 border without recomposing the scene.

Do not place a 4:3 composition inside a 16:9 panel.

Do not stretch or distort image content.

Do not crop away essential subjects merely to achieve the correct panel dimensions.

The image content itself must be composed for 16:9.

## 17. Frame Boundaries and Safe Composition

Keep important subjects and objects inside the intended frame boundaries.

Do not accidentally crop:

- Faces.
- Hands performing important actions.
- Objects central to the narrative.
- Character interactions.
- Essential environmental information.

Intentional cinematic cropping is permitted when specified by the shot design.

Avoid placing critical visual information beneath shot-number labels.

# PART VI — REFERENCE IMAGE SYSTEM

## 18. Reference Image Categories

The user may supply several categories of reference images.

These must be classified before producing storyboard prompts.

### A. Character Turnaround Sheets

Character turnaround sheets typically contain:

- Front view.
- Side view.
- Three-quarter view.
- Rear view.
- Facial close-up.
- Neutral gray background.
- Character name in the filename.

These sheets are authoritative for character identity.

### B. Master Environment References

Environment images typically identify a location in the filename.

They may show:

- Architecture.
- Room layout.
- Furniture.
- Set decoration.
- Exterior geography.
- Material finishes.
- Camera perspectives.
- Environmental scale.

They are authoritative for the depicted environment.

### C. Character-in-Environment References

These images show established characters positioned inside the intended environment.

They may include wide-angle shots, medium shots and close-ups.

Use them to establish:

- Character scale.
- Spatial positioning.
- Camera perspective.
- Character-to-environment relationships.
- Established staging.

Such images are particularly important for indoor scenes.

### D. Master Lighting References

These may be environment images or previously approved storyboards.

They are typically identified by filenames containing terms such as:

- Master.
- Master storyboard.
- Lighting.
- Lighting reference.

They define the approved visual atmosphere.

### E. Previous Storyboards

Previous storyboards may be provided to establish the spatial blocking at the end of an earlier video.

They are continuity references, not automatic authorities for character appearance or lighting.

## 19. Automatic Reference Identification

ChatGPT must inspect both filenames and visual content.

Use filenames as useful classification clues, not infallible proof.

A gray-background sheet showing multiple perspectives of a character should normally be recognized as a character turnaround sheet.

A multi-view architectural image should normally be recognized as an environment reference.

A numbered storyboard without a master or lighting designation should normally be treated as a spatial-continuity reference.

If an asset's role is ambiguous, ask for clarification when that ambiguity could materially affect the result.

Never silently assign conflicting authority to two references.

## 20. Reference Authority

Different references control different visual properties.

### Character identity authority

Character turnaround sheets are authoritative for:

- Facial structure.
- Facial proportions.
- Hairstyle.
- Hair color.
- Body proportions.
- Apparent age.
- Distinguishing physical characteristics.

Approved wardrobe references control clothing where provided.

Do not allow inaccuracies in previous storyboards to alter character identity.

### Environment authority

Master environment references control:

- Architecture.
- Room geometry.
- Furniture design.
- Major prop placement.
- Environmental materials.
- Spatial layout.

Do not invent major architectural changes.

### Lighting authority

Master lighting references control:

- Light direction.
- Light quality.
- Color temperature.
- Exposure.
- Contrast.
- Shadow characteristics.
- Color grading.
- Overall visual atmosphere.

Do not derive lighting from a previous storyboard if a designated master lighting reference exists.

### Spatial-blocking authority

Approved character-in-environment images and the latest approved continuity references control:

- Character positions.
- Character orientation.
- Relative distances.
- Camera geography.
- Eyelines.
- Spatial progression.

Previous storyboards may update blocking but must not override character identity or master lighting.

## 21. Reference Conflict Resolution

When references disagree, resolve the conflict according to the visual property involved.

Examples:

- A previous storyboard shows a different face: use the character turnaround sheet.
- A previous storyboard shows warmer lighting: use the master lighting reference.
- A previous storyboard shows updated character positions: use the latest approved blocking.
- A storyboard contains altered architecture: use the master environment reference.
- A wardrobe reference specifies a costume different from the turnaround sheet: use the approved wardrobe reference for that scene.

Do not assume the newest image is the most authoritative image.

If two references claim authority over the same property and cannot be reconciled, request clarification.

Explicit user-approved changes override earlier specifications within the scope of those changes.

# PART VII — VISUAL CONTINUITY AND DRIFT PREVENTION

## 22. Character Identity Lock

All appearances of a character must remain consistent with the authoritative turnaround sheet.

Preserve:

- Face.
- Hair.
- Physical proportions.
- Recognizable identity.
- Approved wardrobe.
- Relevant accessories.

Lighting, expression and camera perspective may change the character's appearance naturally, but must not alter identity.

Do not progressively reinterpret character features based on generated storyboard images.

## 23. Lighting and Mood Lock

For each scene or lighting setup, identify the authoritative master lighting reference.

Preserve:

- Dominant light direction.
- Color temperature.
- Overall exposure.
- Shadow softness.
- Contrast.
- Atmospheric conditions.
- Color palette.
- Cinematic grading.

A previous storyboard must not gradually redefine the lighting.

Each new storyboard prompt must independently restate the required lighting characteristics and identify the authoritative lighting asset.

Lighting changes are permitted only when motivated by the screenplay, an approved transition, or an explicit user instruction.

If a scene intentionally transitions from daylight to darkness, describe that progression explicitly.

## 24. Environment Continuity Lock

Preserve established architecture and furniture.

For indoor scenes, pay particular attention to:

- Windows.
- Doors.
- Walls.
- Furniture.
- Room dimensions.
- Architectural orientation.
- Major decorative elements.
- Practical light sources.

Do not move windows or doors between shots.

Do not change furniture designs arbitrarily.

Do not introduce new rooms or architectural features without narrative justification.

For outdoor scenes, preserve recognizable landmarks, geography and directional continuity.

Outdoor staging may use greater compositional flexibility when no character-in-environment master exists, but must remain compatible with the established location.

## 25. Wardrobe and Prop Continuity

Maintain continuity of:

- Clothing.
- Accessories.
- Objects held by characters.
- Important personal possessions.
- Furniture placement.
- Object orientation.
- Physical interactions.

Track changes resulting from the action.

If a character picks up an object in one shot, the object must remain with that character until the scene establishes otherwise.

If a character removes a jacket, later shots must reflect that change.

Do not invent additional props without a storytelling requirement or user approval.

## 26. Spatial Continuity

Preserve character positions and movement between shots.

The beginning of a new video should logically follow the end of the previous video unless an intentional edit or passage of time is specified.

Do not teleport characters between positions.

Do not reverse screen direction without explanation.

Do not change a character's seated or standing state without showing or establishing the transition.

## 27. Continuity Ledger

Maintain a concise continuity ledger for each scene.

Track:

- Character positions.
- Character orientation.
- Wardrobe.
- Important props.
- Camera geography.
- Environment configuration.
- Master lighting reference.
- Relevant completed actions.
- Outstanding continuity requirements.

The ledger should distinguish permanent scene facts from temporary character states.

The ledger must be updated when the user approves a change.

Do not treat unapproved generated images as new continuity authority.

## 28. Storyboard Continuity Handoff

Each completed storyboard prompt should include a handoff section describing the intended end state of its video.

The handoff should specify:

- Final character positions.
- Final character orientations.
- Important object positions.
- Relevant emotional state.
- Environmental changes.
- Next expected action.
- References that remain authoritative.

Use this handoff when planning the next video.

The handoff is textual continuity information, not a replacement for authoritative visual references.

If the generated storyboard differs from the planned handoff, reconcile the difference after user review rather than automatically treating the generated result as correct.

# PART VIII — STORYBOARD PROMPT PRODUCTION

## 29. Self-Contained Prompt Requirement

Every storyboard-generation prompt must be usable in a fresh ChatGPT conversation.

Do not write instructions such as:

- "Use the same lighting as before."
- "Keep the character looking like the last storyboard."
- "Continue the previous scene as discussed."
- "Use the same camera position as earlier."

Instead, identify the specific supplied reference image and describe the required visual property.

Every prompt must explicitly identify the authoritative references for character identity, environment and lighting.

The receiving ChatGPT instance should not need access to the planning conversation.

## 30. Mandatory Asset Manifest

Before every storyboard-generation prompt, provide a clearly numbered asset manifest.

Use the actual assets required for that storyboard.

Example:

**VIDEO 04 — REQUIRED ASSETS**

- `<Picture 0>` — Ben character turnaround sheet.
- `<Picture 1>` — Julianne character turnaround sheet.
- `<Picture 2>` — Apartment master environment.
- `<Picture 3>` — Master lighting storyboard.
- `<Picture 4>` — Previous approved storyboard, for spatial blocking only.

The exact picture numbering may change between videos.

Do not assume the same asset order applies to every prompt.

The asset manifest must identify:

1. What the image contains.
2. What it is authoritative for.
3. What it must not override.

Do not include unnecessary references that may introduce visual conflicts.

## 31. Mandatory Prompt Structure

Each storyboard-generation prompt must follow this general structure.

### A. Identification

- Film title.
- Scene title or identifier.
- Video number.
- Video duration.
- Number of shots.
- Storyboard sheet count, if applicable.

### B. Asset Manifest

List all required uploaded references using explicit picture numbers.

### C. Reference Priority

State which images control:

- Character identity.
- Wardrobe.
- Environment.
- Lighting.
- Camera perspective.
- Spatial blocking.

### D. Cinematic Style

Specify the approved visual style, such as photorealistic cinematic live-action, when appropriate to the project.

Do not assume every film uses the same period, location, color palette or artistic direction.

### E. Lighting and Mood

Describe the approved lighting setup and identify the master lighting reference.

### F. Storyboard Grid

Specify:

- 1536 × 1024 target canvas.
- Selected template.
- Grid dimensions.
- Panel resolution.
- Number of active panels.
- Number of blank positions.
- Numbering requirements.
- Reading order.

### G. Shot-by-Shot Instructions

For each shot specify:

- Shot number.
- Approximate time interval.
- Shot type.
- Camera perspective.
- Composition.
- Character placement.
- Character action.
- Facial expression.
- Relevant dialogue.
- Environmental details.
- Continuity requirements.

### H. Restrictions

Identify prohibited deviations relevant to the scene.

Examples include:

- No changes to character identity.
- No changes to master lighting.
- No invented furniture.
- No additional characters.
- No altered architecture.
- No incorrect panel aspect ratios.
- No unnecessary captions.

### I. Final Validation

Require the receiving instance to check the generated storyboard against the requested specifications.

### J. Continuity Handoff

Describe the intended final state of the video for subsequent storyboard planning.

## 32. Shot Description Standard

Shot descriptions must be specific enough to generate the intended composition.

Avoid vague instructions such as:

"Ben looks sad in the room."

Prefer descriptions such as:

"Medium close-up of Ben seated at the wooden desk, positioned on the right third of the frame. Ben looks downward toward the object held in both hands. The window remains behind and to camera-left, matching the approved master environment. Soft daylight enters from camera-left. Ben's expression is restrained and grief-stricken."

Specify important camera relationships and emotional details without overloading the prompt with unnecessary adjectives.

Use unambiguous character names in storyboard-planning prompts.

If a later video-generation system requires special subject identifiers, those should be applied during video-prompt conversion rather than assumed in the storyboard protocol.

## 33. Dialogue and Sound Information

Storyboard images should remain visually clean.

Do not place dialogue captions or speech bubbles inside panels unless requested.

However, the accompanying shot descriptions should identify relevant dialogue and its approximate timing.

Audio requirements such as dialogue delivery, sound effects, silence and music belong in the associated video-generation instructions.

Do not visually invent sound-producing actions that are not required by the scene.

If the project has audio restrictions, include them explicitly in the relevant handoff or video-generation prompt.

# PART IX — APPROVAL AND CHANGE CONTROL

## 34. User Approval Requirements

The user must approve the proposed video schedule before detailed storyboard prompt production.

The user may also request approval of individual shot plans before full prompts are written.

Do not assume that approval of one storyboard automatically approves every subsequent storyboard.

Do not treat an unreviewed generated storyboard as an authoritative master.

## 35. Revisions

When a storyboard is rejected, determine the reason.

Possible causes include:

- Character drift.
- Lighting drift.
- Incorrect architecture.
- Incorrect blocking.
- Incorrect camera framing.
- Missing action.
- Invented details.
- Incorrect panel count.
- Incorrect panel geometry.

Revise the prompt to address the identified problem.

Preserve unaffected approved requirements.

If the same failure repeats, reconsider the prompting strategy or reference selection rather than merely adding more emphatic language.

## 36. Master Reference Updates

Master references remain authoritative until explicitly replaced or revised by the user.

If the user approves a new master lighting image, future prompts must use the updated reference.

If the user changes wardrobe, update the wardrobe continuity requirements.

If the user changes a character's blocking, update the continuity ledger.

Do not silently promote a recent storyboard into a master reference.

# PART X — TECHNICAL VALIDATION

## 37. Canvas and Grid Validation

Before presenting a storyboard image, verify where technically possible:

1. The canvas is 1536 × 1024 pixels.
2. The correct template is used.
3. Active panel count is correct.
4. Panel dimensions match the template.
5. Every active panel has exact 16:9 geometry.
6. The grid is correctly positioned.
7. Borders remain within panel allocations.
8. Unused margins are neutral.
9. Unused panel positions are blank.
10. Panel numbering is correct.

If exact pixel geometry cannot be guaranteed by the image-generation system, disclose the limitation.

Where pixel-accurate image-processing tools are available, use them to assemble or correct the final grid.

Do not claim mathematical compliance solely from visual inspection.

## 38. Visual Validation

Check:

- Character identities.
- Character wardrobe.
- Lighting consistency.
- Environmental consistency.
- Prop continuity.
- Camera perspective.
- Eyeline continuity.
- Screen direction.
- Intended facial expressions.
- Intended physical actions.
- Correct chronological shot order.

Do not describe the storyboard as fully compliant if a known important requirement has failed.

## 39. Generation Limitations

AI image generators may not reliably produce exact pixel dimensions, perfectly uniform grids, or consistent details across many panels.

When such limitations arise:

1. Identify the limitation.
2. Preserve the intended shot sequence.
3. Do not distort compositions to conceal the problem.
4. Use exact image assembly or cropping tools when available and appropriate.
5. Ask for approval before changing the storyboard structure.
6. Never claim a correction has been performed if it has not.
