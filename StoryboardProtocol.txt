MASTER STORYBOARD PRODUCTION PROTOCOL
**Version:** 2.2 — Cinematography, Visual Storytelling, Integrated Lighting + Spatial Continuity Edition
**Purpose:** Narrative-led cinematography and shot design, editorial continuity, dimensional character lighting, text-based spatial continuity handoffs, and self-contained image-generation prompts
**Target storyboard canvas:** 1536 × 1024 pixels
**Panel aspect ratio:** Exact 16:9 for every active panel; no merged or expanded panels
**Maximum AI video segment:** 15 seconds
1. Governing objective
Create storyboard plans and prompts for **ultra photorealistic, theatrical, cinematic** live-action imagery. Every active storyboard panel must resemble a believable frame photographed or filmed with a real camera in a real physical environment, not a drawing, concept-art painting, illustration, synthetic render, or stylized mock-up. Storyboards are visual targets for eventual AI video generation: composition, atmosphere, lighting, materials, and character appearance must be production-ready.
**Foundational rule:** Dramatic intention determines camera placement, shot selection, movement, composition, lighting and cuts; storytelling determines the shots; the grid accommodates them; authoritative references preserve identity and geography; approved lighting geography remains consistent while character illumination is intentionally sculpted; each prompt stands on its own.
2. Two independent roles
**Role A — Planning ChatGPT:** Read this protocol and project materials, estimate natural performance duration, propose video segmentation, design shots, classify references, review previously generated storyboard images supplied by the user, extract final-panel spatial continuity in text, maintain continuity, and produce complete standalone storyboard-generation prompts and asset manifests. Do not generate storyboard images unless explicitly asked.
**Role B — Image-generation ChatGPT:** In a fresh conversation, receive one self-contained storyboard prompt, its written final-panel spatial continuity handoff (for subsequent videos), and its listed authoritative image assets; produce the requested storyboard without redesigning the approved sequence or changing visual authorities. The previous storyboard image is NOT supplied by default. Ask about missing essential assets or conflicts instead of improvising. Do not assume access to Role A's conversation.
3. Mandatory workflow and approvals
Stage A — Scene analysis
Read the full scene, script, dialogue, actions, transitions, locations, and emotional beats. Mentally stage a plausible live-action performance. Account for natural dialogue pace, listening, pauses, reactions, physical movement, prop handling, silence, and camera movement. Estimate realistic runtime rather than forcing an arbitrary duration.
Stage B — Video-generation schedule
Propose the **total scene runtime** and a table of numbered videos with **duration (1–15 seconds each)** and **one- or two-sentence summaries** of what happens in each. Sum the segment durations to the proposed runtime; identify any non-generated transitions separately. For long or multi-location scenes, first explain the overall dramatic structure. Segment at natural action or dialogue boundaries; avoid cutting mid-sentence or mid-action without an intentional edit. **Stop and obtain user approval before detailed storyboard prompt generation.**
Stage C — Cinematography design and shot planning
First establish the sequence-level visual strategy and the dramatic purpose of each camera setup using Section 4.3B. Design coverage and intended edit points before choosing grid layouts. For each approved video, determine its shot count based only on narrative needs, not grid capacity. Choose camera size, angle, movement, blocking, expressions, relevant dialogue, approximate time intervals, and continuity. One 15-second video may be one continuous shot or many shots. A continuous moving shot need not be subdivided merely to increase panels. If multiple keyframes are necessary to convey camera motion, label them as movement keyframes, not editorial cuts.
Stage D — Prompt production
Write one complete, standalone storyboard image-generation prompt per video, including its asset manifest, explicit reference authority, ultra-photorealistic cinematic visual directive, environment lighting lock, shot-specific character lighting plan, exact grid, shot-by-shot framing and timing, mandatory cinematographic intent and editorial relationship, prohibitions, and continuity handoff. For every video after the first, embed a text-only final-panel spatial continuity record derived by Role A from the previous storyboard; do not include the previous storyboard image in the Role B asset manifest unless the exception in Section 17.5 is invoked. Do not use vague phrases such as “same as before.”
Stage E — Generation, review, approval
The user provides each prompt and required assets to a fresh image-generation conversation. Check the result for narrative-led camera placement, motivated movement, sequence coverage, editorial continuity, story, identity, environment, lighting, cinematic realism, strict 16:9 geometry, numbering, absence of captions/text, and continuity. A generated image is **not authoritative until approved**. On revision, preserve unaffected approved details. If the same failure repeats, revise reference selection, composition, or production method instead of simply adding stronger adjectives.
4. Mandatory visual realism standard
4.1 Required language
Every storyboard image-generation prompt must prominently include the exact words **“ultra photorealistic, theatrical, cinematic”** in its primary visual-style directive. These words are mandatory, but are not a substitute for specific photographic instructions.
Use this baseline directive, tailored to the scene:
> Generate an **ultra photorealistic, theatrical, cinematic** live-action storyboard. Each panel must look like a genuine frame captured by a professional cinematographer with a physical camera, real-world lenses, believable practical sets, natural materials, physically plausible lighting, and consistent film-grade color treatment. The image must be convincing as a still from the intended finished movie, not an illustration, concept art, video-game render, or artificial-looking AI image. Preserve all authoritative character, environment, and master-lighting references.
4.2 Photographic plausibility
Use credible lens perspective, camera height, field of view, depth cues, realistic exposure, dynamic range, natural highlight roll-off, physically believable shadows, and appropriate reflections. Faces must retain authentic anatomy, subtle asymmetry, realistic skin texture, and natural eyes and hair. Materials must have believable texture and reflectance: fabric looks like fabric, wood like wood, glass like glass, and metal like metal. Keep environmental details grounded in the reference images.
Do not invent specific camera brands, focal lengths, apertures, film stocks, or LUTs unless they help the approved composition or are provided in the project package. Such parameters may be suggested as photographic guidance, not presented as facts about an actual camera capture.
4.3 Cinematic lighting and color
Light should feel motivated by believable sources such as windows, daylight, practical lamps, streetlights, or established cinematic fixtures. Preserve the master lighting reference's direction, color temperature, contrast, exposure, shadow softness, atmosphere, and grade. Create rich, nuanced tonal separation and believable skin tones without changing the approved mood. Avoid arbitrary orange-and-teal grading, excessively saturated colors, crushed blacks, clipped highlights, unnaturally glowing edges, and overprocessed HDR.
**An explicitly approved master lighting design overrides generic cinematic-style preferences.** However, an environment or architecture master is not automatically a binding facial-lighting master. Apply the authority distinctions and dual-layer lighting rules in Section 4.3A. “Make it cinematic” never authorizes a different time of day, unmotivated light source, color palette, or emotional atmosphere.

4.3A INTEGRATED CINEMATIC LIGHTING AND FACIAL SHADOW SYSTEM — MANDATORY

4.3A.1 Objective and terminology
Design each shot as a plausible frame from a professionally lit live-action theatrical film, with dimensional facial modelling, readable performance, believable environmental illumination, and deliberate contrast. The goal is NOT to darken every frame or make every face half black. The goal is controlled directional light, sculpted shadows, and dramatic intention.
SHORT LIGHTING: the side of the face turned toward the camera is comparatively darker, while the far side receives more key light. This is the preferred treatment for many dramatic medium shots and close-ups, where physically and narratively appropriate. BROAD LIGHTING: the camera-facing side receives more illumination; acceptable when motivated or aesthetically useful. NEGATIVE FILL: subtraction or blocking of unwanted reflected light on the shadow side, not a negative-valued light source. REMBRANDT LIGHTING: a directional key may form a small light triangle on the shadow cheek; use selectively, never as an obligatory geometric stamp.

4.3A.2 TWO-LAYER LIGHTING DESIGN — ENVIRONMENT AND CHARACTER
Layer E — ENVIRONMENT LIGHTING: establishes the visible set, window and practical positions, time of day, exterior conditions, broad ambient level, color palette, atmosphere, exposure relationship, and the continuity of light sources across shots. The environment master controls architecture and source geography. An approved lighting master also controls its expressly approved lighting properties.
Layer C — CHARACTER LIGHTING: shapes the light that reaches performers' faces and bodies through camera position, blocking, orientation, motivated key placement, diffusion, flags, negative fill, bounce control, selective soft fill, and subtle motivated separation. The character can have richer facial contrast than the background without looking disconnected from it. A cinematographer may shape off-camera light or subtract bounce, provided its apparent direction and color remain consistent with the established world.
Never imply that an on-screen lamp is the sole source of all illumination when a window or plausible off-camera extension of that lamp could account for the lighting. Never create an impossible key direction, contradictory shadows, or unexplained glowing rim. Character lighting is a controlled extension of the scene's lighting geography, not a separate fantasy light rig.

4.3A.3 REFERENCE AUTHORITY AND CONFLICT RESOLUTION
Classify each supplied reference by what it is intended to control, rather than treating every bright face in every image as a mandatory lighting design.
A. Environment/architecture master: authoritative for layout, windows, furniture, surface materials, and visible practical sources; its incidental facial flatness does not prohibit improved facial modelling.
B. Explicitly approved lighting master: authoritative for approved time of day, source direction, atmosphere, color temperature, and intended contrast/lighting treatment. Preserve it; if a requested dramatic facial-lighting change truly conflicts, ask for approval to revise or establish a new lighting master.
C. Character turnaround/wardrobe: authoritative for identity and clothing, NOT for neutral studio illumination or background.
D. Previous approved storyboard (reviewed by Role A): informs the written spatial continuity handoff and the scene lighting ledger, but is not routinely uploaded to Role B. Its approved blocking and expressly approved lighting decisions control continuity without overriding higher-authority masters. See Section 17.5.
E. New scene without a lighting master: propose a plausible environment lighting blueprint and a character-lighting blueprint together; label both as provisional until approved.
Where a reference is ambiguous, identify the ambiguity and ask only if it materially affects the result. Do not silently reverse sunlight, change time of day, or transform an explicitly approved high-key look into noir.

4.3A.4 PREFERRED SHORT LIGHTING AND NEGATIVE-SIDE PRESENTATION
For close-ups, medium close-ups, and character-centered medium shots, prefer a three-quarter face orientation and camera placement that present the comparatively darker, camera-near cheek while the far cheek receives a motivated key. State the camera/face/key relationship explicitly. Preserve enough eye, mouth, and skin detail to read the performance. Use negative fill or controlled bounce to retain shadow depth where appropriate. Avoid default flat front fill, beauty-ring illumination, symmetrical facial brightness, and automatic HDR shadow lifting.
Do not force short lighting in full frontal views, wide shots where faces are tiny, moving shots, backlit silhouettes, or reverse angles where it would break continuity. Camera and blocking may be chosen to favor it before the shot is locked, but no arbitrary lighting flip is permitted after the scene geography is established.

4.3A.5 REMBRANDT, SIDE LIGHT, BACKLIGHT AND EXCEPTIONS
Use Rembrandt-style lighting selectively for tense conversations, revelations, grief, or moral ambiguity when the actor's head angle and source placement permit a subtle triangle on the shadow cheek. Use side lighting for sharper tension, soft directional lighting for intimacy, and motivated backlight or edge separation for spatial clarity. Broad lighting and frontal illumination remain valid when emotionally intentional, for example an open or reassuring moment. A cinematic film does not require every face to be short-lit or every shot to have a Rembrandt triangle.

4.3A.6 FACIAL MODELLING AND PHOTOREALISM
Specify natural shadows cast by nose, brow, eye sockets, cheekbones, jawline, and neck; preserve realistic skin texture, specular response, reflected color, subtle asymmetry, and soft-to-hard transitions appropriate to source size. Avoid an artificial hard split painted onto the face, pitch-black eyes without intention, glowing skin, beauty-filter smoothing, false edge highlights, or extreme local-contrast processing. Keep shadow-side detail appropriate to the mood and final video readability. Use controlled highlights and natural roll-off; avoid clipped windows and crushed blacks unless deliberate and approved.

4.3A.7 SHOT-LEVEL LIGHTING SPECIFICATION — REQUIRED
Every shot with a discernible face must contain a named 'CINEMATOGRAPHY — LIGHTING DESIGN' subfield describing: (1) motivated source and source geography; (2) key direction relative to camera AND face; (3) illuminated facial planes and shadow-side planes; (4) desired short/broad/Rembrandt/side/backlit treatment if applicable; (5) negative fill, bounce, and fill strategy; (6) contrast, shadow softness, exposure, and eye readability; (7) emotional purpose; and (8) continuity with prior and next shots. For wide shots without readable facial features, specify environment lighting and silhouette/body separation instead. Do not substitute 'cinematic lighting' or 'dramatic shadows' for these decisions.

4.3A.8 SHOT-TO-SHOT LIGHTING GEOGRAPHY
Maintain a scene lighting ledger: window and practical positions, sun direction, source colors, character positions, camera axis, intended key side, shadow-side detail, and approved exposure/grade. On reverse angles, the camera-facing cheek may not always be dark; use plausible blocking, motivated bounce control, or accept a different facial-lighting pattern rather than teleporting the key. A head turn can change which cheek is illuminated; reflect that change naturally. Moving characters may cross pools of light. Lighting evolution must follow the blocking and established sources.

4.3A.9 SCENE-ADAPTIVE LIGHTING
Dialogue/confrontation: controlled short lighting and moderate-to-strong shadow modelling when motivated.
Emotional intimacy or parent-child tenderness: soft directional light and gentle, readable shadows; avoid gratuitously sinister darkness.
Daylight interiors: window-motivated key, selective negative fill, ambient bounce controlled without turning the room artificially dark.
Night interiors: practical-motivated pools of light, selective face exposure, believable falloff.
Day exteriors: sunlight/skylight direction, shadow shape, flags or bounce where plausible; do not invent studio fixtures in frame.
Action: dimensional faces but readable hands, bodies, eyelines, choreography, hazards, and spatial geography; clarity outranks darkness.
Suspense: selective visibility and deeper shadows when dramatically justified, while preserving continuity and key narrative information.

4.3A.10 IMAGE-GENERATION AND VIDEO HANDOFF
The planning ChatGPT must embed the actual shot-specific lighting design inside each standalone image-generation prompt, not merely refer to this protocol. When reviewing the storyboard, check whether faces are unintentionally flat, whether the key direction is physically consistent, and whether background and face lighting belong to the same scene. In later Seedance/MiniMax conversions, carry over the approved key direction, shadow distribution, practical-source geography, and contrast; do not let the video model flatten the face through invented ambient fill. This protocol governs storyboard planning; a separate video-conversion protocol may add model-specific preservation instructions.

4.3A.11 LIGHTING QUALITY GATES AND REVISION POLICY
CRITICAL: unmotivated or reversed key direction that breaks a master/adjacent shot; facial shadows inconsistent with the established source; severe loss of facial readability or required action; wholesale flattening of a dramatically lit approved shot; lighting changes that alter story-critical time or mood. Reject or correct.
MINOR/ACCEPTABLE: slightly different shadow-edge softness, imperfect Rembrandt triangle, subtle bounced-fill changes, modest contrast variation that preserves intended dimensionality and continuity. Do not waste generations correcting these alone.
If a shot is flat, revise the source-camera-face geometry, fill/negative-fill description, or reference classification; do not merely add 'more cinematic' adjectives. Review each panel separately.

4.3A.12 EXAMPLE — OFFICE MEDIUM CLOSE-UP
'CINEMATOGRAPHY — LIGHTING DESIGN: The established camera-right window is the primary soft daylight source. Frame the seated character in a plausible three-quarter orientation so the far cheek and temple receive the key and the camera-facing cheek remains in a soft, detailed shadow (short lighting). Reduce unwanted white-wall bounce with conceptual negative fill; retain readable shadow-side eye and natural skin texture. The warm desk lamp remains a subtle background practical, not an unexplained frontal face light. Preserve window position, office architecture, time of day, and approved grade. Do not reverse the window light merely to obtain a prettier portrait.'


4.3B NARRATIVE-LED CINEMATOGRAPHY AND EDITORIAL DESIGN — MANDATORY

4.3B.1 Governing principle
A storyboard is a plan for a directed sequence, not a gallery of attractive stills. Every shot must justify what the camera reveals, conceals, emphasizes or allows the audience to feel. Photographic beauty alone does not justify a shot. Cinematography planning belongs to Role A BEFORE Role B generates images. Storytelling, performance, clarity and continuity take priority over visual spectacle; deliberate spectacle remains welcome when motivated.

4.3B.2 Sequence-level visual strategy — required before individual shot descriptions
For each scene or substantial dramatic beat, Role A determines: (a) audience knowledge and emotional objective; (b) dominant perspective or point of view; (c) initial and final visual distance/intimacy; (d) action axis and spatial geography; (e) coverage and intended edit points; (f) when camera movement is justified and when stillness is stronger; (g) visual motif or composition changes that track the dramatic arc; (h) environmental and facial lighting continuity. This planning may be concise but must be concrete. Do not introduce extra shots merely to demonstrate different camera techniques.

4.3B.3 Mandatory shot-level CINEMATOGRAPHIC INTENT
For every shot, include a named 'CINEMATOGRAPHIC INTENT & CAMERA DESIGN' field in the WRITTEN prompt (never printed on the image), stating: (1) narrative/emotional purpose; (2) whose viewpoint or dramatic alignment is privileged; (3) shot size, angle, height, and approximate camera location relative to characters and fixed landmarks; (4) composition including subject placement, headroom, look room, foreground/midground/background; (5) lens perspective and focus/depth treatment only where useful; (6) camera behavior — locked, pan, tilt, track, push, pull, handheld or orbit — with its motivation, start/end framing, and real-time pacing; (7) relation to previous and following shots, including intended cut, screen direction, eyeline and change of information; (8) compatibility with the approved lighting and reference geography. Use precise observable instructions rather than vague adjectives. A static shot is an active choice, not an omission.

4.3B.4 Shot size and perspective as narrative tools
Wide shots establish geography, isolation, scale, or changing relationships. Medium and two-shots show interactions and physical performance. Over-the-shoulder views create alignment and clarify conversational geography. Close-ups reveal emotion or decisive information. Extreme close-ups and inserts emphasize story-critical details only when the audience needs them. POV is used when the character's subjective view matters. Reaction shots are included when the reaction changes our understanding, not as automatic filler. Choose shot sizes based on narrative need, not a quota or a requirement to alternate mechanically.

4.3B.5 Camera movement restraint and motivation
Do not default to perpetual slow dolly moves, floating gimbal motion, repeated arcs or orbiting for visual decoration. Every move needs a reason: follow physical action, disclose information, shift allegiance, intensify tension, expose a spatial relationship, or punctuate a major action beat. Specify starting composition, movement path, subject relative motion, and ending composition. Prefer a locked camera for a powerful performance, suspenseful hold, or clear physical action when movement would distract. Do not prescribe slow motion through camera language; performance remains at natural real-time speed unless the screenplay explicitly calls for slow motion.

4.3B.6 Spatial and editorial grammar
Establish the action axis and preserve screen direction, eyelines, entrances/exits, object positions, and camera-side geography across adjacent shots. Cross the axis only through a motivated neutral/on-axis shot, visible camera move, re-establishing angle, or another clear editorial transition. Plan cuts on action, reaction, revelation, or meaningful changes of perspective. Avoid accidental jump cuts caused by near-identical framings and abrupt shifts in scale or position. Distinguish a new shot from a movement keyframe in one continuous shot. Do not duplicate an identical opening frame across consecutive video segments unless the continuity requirement genuinely calls for it. Continuity handoffs preserve blocking without locking the next camera to the previous angle.

4.3B.7 Composition, layers, optics and dimensionality
Compose deliberately using positive/negative space, asymmetry when useful, leading lines, occlusion, foreground frames, background landmarks and controlled subject separation. Maintain believable physical camera positions and lens perspective; avoid impossible camera placements, exaggerated perspective, synthetic shallow-focus cutouts, or bokeh that hides important action. A deep-focus wide shot can be more cinematic than a blurred close-up. Camera-side and face-lighting choices must be reconciled with Section 4.3A, not independently optimized into contradictory setups.

4.3B.8 Coverage for dialogue and revelation
Design an intelligible master or spatial anchor when needed, then select coverage that tracks who holds power, what information is revealed, and which reaction matters. Vary shot scale and angle only when meaningful. Maintain the existing visible-speaker dialogue rule in Section 17.6; do not create reaction-only coverage that would require off-screen speech. Allow stillness and silence. Reserve a tighter frame or camera move for a genuine emotional or informational turn.

4.3B.9 Coverage for action and magic
Establish the physical geography before close coverage. Preserve readable full-body movement, contact points, screen direction, and believable reaction/recovery. Use wider frames to show choreography and close-ups/inserts for decisive strikes, hands, objects, and consequences. Dynamic tracking and camera orbits, including during a flying kick or spectacular magic beat, are encouraged when the action remains intelligible, camera path is physically plausible, and the effect merits emphasis. Do not obscure the actual impact or replace choreography with rapid decorative cuts. Keep real-time movement and any separate combat-engine restrictions intact.

4.3B.10 Visual progression and variation across a sequence
Judge whether the sequence develops visually: for example, establish distance, move closer as tension rises, then widen to reveal a changed power relationship. Such progression is illustrative, not compulsory. Vary composition, scale, perspective, motion and stillness in response to dramatic beats. Avoid uniformly centered portraits, repeated medium-shot coverage, indiscriminate shallow focus and identical camera movement in every video. Visual continuity does not require repetitive framing.

4.3B.11 Required prompt handoff and storyboard review
Role A must write the actual shot-level cinematographic instructions in every standalone Role B prompt; never assume Role B knows this protocol. Include a short 'SEQUENCE CINEMATOGRAPHY STRATEGY' before the shot list when useful. Review storyboard panels both individually AND as a planned edit: ask whether the audience can understand space, whether angles cut together, whether the framing supports the intended dramatic beat, and whether camera movement is motivated. The storyboard is a still-image representation of proposed shots: motion paths, edits and timing must remain explicit in the written shot plan for downstream video generation.

4.3B.12 Cinematography quality gates
CRITICAL: unclear action geography or broken screen direction; an impossible or contradictory camera position; missing narrative-critical information; an editorial mismatch that makes a sequence unintelligible; a camera setup that violates authoritative architecture; camera coverage that cannot satisfy the visible-speaker rule. Correct before approval. MAJOR: arbitrary repetitive camera moves, unmotivated framing that obscures the performance, monotonous coverage where a key dramatic change needs a new perspective, a planned cut that produces a distracting jump. Revise where materially harmful. MINOR: a slightly different motivated crop, modest lens-perspective variation, or aesthetic preference that does not compromise story or continuity. Do not waste generations chasing stylistic perfection.

4.3B.13 Example — confrontation
'SEQUENCE CINEMATOGRAPHY STRATEGY: Begin with a restrained two-shot establishing table, door and the characters' separation. Preserve the established axis. Move to a shoulder-level angle aligned with the questioning character for the first revelation; use a locked close-up on the responding character when the information lands. Cut to the listener's reaction only after the speaker finishes the line, preserving visible-speaker dialogue. Keep camera moves rare and motivated by a shift in power.'
'SHOT 2 — CINEMATOGRAPHIC INTENT & CAMERA DESIGN: Audience aligns with the questioner. Medium over-the-shoulder from the established near side of the table, with a soft foreground shoulder and the respondent on the far third; preserve the lamp and doorway as spatial anchors. Camera locked. Natural perspective; eyes in focus; background still legible. Cut from the establishing two-shot after the questioner completes the line. The respondent is visibly speaking throughout the response. Follow with a tighter shot only when the revelation changes the emotional stakes. Apply the shot-specific lighting design separately under Section 4.3A.7.'

4.4 Depth of field and optics
Use shallow depth of field **when appropriate** to isolate a face, reaction, or narrative object; use deeper focus when geography, group blocking, or environmental storytelling must remain visible. Maintain believable focus planes and natural background blur. Avoid indiscriminate bokeh, artificial miniature effects, extreme blur, and lens effects that conceal essential staging. Do not sacrifice clear shot communication for photographic decoration.
4.5 Prohibited artificial appearance
Avoid painterly or illustrative textures, CGI/game-engine appearance, plastic skin, waxy faces, excessive skin smoothing, uncanny facial proportions, overly sharpened micro-contrast, unrealistic glow, fake volumetric beams, implausible reflections, random lens flares, exaggerated vignette, excessive grain, surreal colors, and unnecessary stylization. Do not add atmospheric effects such as smoke, fog, rain, or floating particles unless supported by the script or approved references.
4.6 Consistency across all panels and videos
All panels in one scene must appear to belong to the same professionally photographed film, with coherent production design, camera treatment, lighting, and color. Shot-to-shot variation in focal length, depth of field, framing, or exposure is allowed when dramatically justified, but must not produce a different visual identity. **Never use the latest generated storyboard as a replacement for the master lighting or character references.**
4.7 Realism verification
Before approval, inspect whether each panel plausibly resembles an actual live-action film frame; whether faces, hands, materials, shadows, perspective, and focus appear physically credible; and whether the intended mood and master grade are intact. A panel that is technically 16:9 but looks like illustration or synthetic CGI fails the visual standard. Do not claim a result is photorealistic merely because the prompt used the required keywords.
5. Cinematic shot design
Use appropriate variety among extreme wide, wide, medium-wide, medium, medium close-up, close-up, extreme close-up, two-shot, over-the-shoulder, POV, reaction, insert, low-angle, and high-angle shots. Variety must serve storytelling; do not force every type into each video. Wide shots establish geography, medium shots convey action, close-ups convey emotion, and inserts reveal essential details. Preserve the 180-degree axis, eyelines, left/right screen direction, object placement, and camera geography unless an intentional, clearly established crossing is approved. Avoid unexplained teleports or changes in standing/seated state.
6. Fixed canvas and mandatory templates
**Target canvas:** 1536 × 1024 (3:2). **All active panels:** exact 16:9, equal size within a sheet, no stretching or distortion. Select the smallest available capacity that fits the shot count, subject to the approved dense-grid exception.
| Shots required | Template capacity | Columns × rows | Panel pixels | Grid pixels | Centered margins (L/R; T/B) |
|---|---:|---|---|---|---|
| 1–4 | 4 | 2 × 2 | 768 × 432 | 1536 × 864 | 0/0; 80/80 |
| 5–6 | 6 | 2 × 3 | 592 × 333 | 1184 × 999 | 176/176; 12/13 |
| 7–8 | 8 | 2 × 4 | 448 × 252 | 896 × 1008 | 320/320; 8/8 |
| 9–12 | 12 | 3 × 4 | 448 × 252 | 1344 × 1008 | 96/96; 8/8 |
| 13–16 | 16 | 4 × 4 | 384 × 216 | 1536 × 864 | 0/0; 80/80 |
All panel dimensions listed above are mathematically exact 16:9 (including 592 × 333). Center grids on the canvas. Use uniform neutral margins. Draw thin, consistent borders **inside** allocated panel rectangles; do not enlarge the grid or shrink active image content unpredictably.
**CRITICAL GEOMETRY RULE:** Every active image rectangle must occupy exactly ONE grid cell and must be 16:9. Never combine two cells into a panoramic panel, expand the final panel across an unused row, or stretch a frame to consume spare space. For odd shot counts (3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 15), leave all unused grid cells blank at their original cell dimensions. A 3-shot storyboard is three separate 768 × 432 images in a 2 × 2 grid, with the bottom-right cell empty; the third image must NEVER span the bottom row. This is a critical downstream video-generation requirement, not a cosmetic preference.

**Panel count is never dictated by capacity.** A sequence may contain any number of shots, including odd numbers. Do not add filler shots or delete meaningful shots to fit a template.
6.1 Blank positions
Unused panel positions retain their dimensions and borders but have uniform neutral fill, no imagery, no shot numbers, no text, and no meaning as video pauses. Example: 7 shots use the 8-panel grid with the eighth position blank.
6.2 Numbering
Number active panels sequentially in reading order, left-to-right then top-to-bottom. Use only small, unobtrusive numeric shot identifiers at the upper-left of active panels, without obscuring essential action. No captions, dialogue, speech bubbles, subtitles, action descriptions, timestamps, scene explanations, text beneath panels, or other written annotations anywhere on the storyboard sheet. Do not reserve any caption strip or text area. If numeric identifiers are not required by the user, omit those too.
**Restart shot numbering at 1 for every new video generation.** For example, Video 04 can contain shots 1–7 and Video 05 can contain shots 1–4. If a **single video** spans multiple storyboard sheets, continue that video's shot numbering across those sheets. Keep video numbers distinct from shot numbers.
6.3 Dense grids and multiple sheets
If a 12–16 panel grid would compromise facial readability or important details, ChatGPT **may recommend** splitting into multiple sheets. Explain the proposed distribution and benefits, but **do not split without explicit user approval**. A sequence requiring more than 16 panels necessarily uses multiple sheets; preserve chronological order and shot numbering within the same video. Each sheet retains the 1536 × 1024 target canvas.
7. Full-frame composition
Each active panel must show the intended **complete, independent 16:9 video frame**, including left, right, top, and bottom boundaries. Compose the actual image for widescreen; do not place a 4:3, square, or portrait composition inside a 16:9 border or crop essential action merely to make the panel fit. Preserve camera perspective, subject scale, environment, blocking, and background. Intentional close cropping is allowed when specified. Do not place essential subjects under number labels. Never create a panorama or use a wider-than-16:9 image to depict action: recompose the shot within a single 16:9 cell instead. Never rely on the downstream video generator to crop or repair panel geometry.
8. Reference image categories and identification
Inspect **both filename and visual content** before classifying assets; filenames are clues, not guarantees.
- **Character turnaround sheets:** Usually gray background, multiple views (front, side, three-quarter, rear, face close-up), character name in filename. Authoritative for face, hair, physique, apparent age, defining features, and identity.
- **Wardrobe references:** Authoritative for approved costumes and accessories when separate from turnaround sheets.
- **Master environment references:** Usually named by location; authoritative for architecture, furnishings, major props, materials, and spatial layout.
- **Character-in-environment masters:** Wide/medium/close views establishing character scale, camera perspectives, and staging; particularly important indoors.
- **Master lighting image/storyboard:** Often named “master” or “lighting”; authoritative for illumination, exposure, atmosphere, color temperature, shadows, and grade, even if blocking is outdated.
- **Previous numbered storyboard:** Supplied to Role A for review and text-based extraction of latest **approved** spatial blocking, eyelines, camera geography, and interaction continuity. Do NOT routinely include it among Role B image uploads. Not authoritative for character identity; see Section 17.5 for a limited visual-reference exception.
If roles are unclear or assets conflict on the same visual property, ask for clarification. Do not silently choose. An explicit user-approved change supersedes older guidance **only for the affected property**.
9. Authority hierarchy by property
**Character identity:** Turnaround sheets override interpretations in storyboards or environmental images.
**Wardrobe:** Approved scene-specific costume references override default clothing depicted on character sheets.
**Architecture and furnishings:** Master environment images override accidental changes in later storyboards.
**Lighting and color:** Explicitly approved master lighting designs override later storyboard drift and generic “cinematic” styling. Environment masters control source geography and atmosphere but do not automatically lock incidental flat facial illumination. Apply the dual-layer authority rules in Section 4.3A.
**Spatial blocking:** Latest approved staging, communicated to Role B primarily through Role A’s written final-panel spatial continuity record, controls current positions without changing higher-authority identity, architecture, or lighting. The previous storyboard image is not a default Role B asset.
**Camera composition:** Approved shot plan controls framing, informed by camera reference images.
Do not treat the most recently generated image as automatically most authoritative. No unapproved image can establish a new master.
10. Continuity locks
Maintain a **character identity lock** (face, hair, proportions, approved clothing); **environment lock** (doors, windows, walls, furniture, major props); **lighting/mood lock** (direction, color, exposure, contrast, shadow, grade); **spatial lock** (character positions, orientation, eyelines, screen direction); and **prop/wardrobe lock** (objects held, transferred, placed, worn, or removed).
Indoor environments demand especially careful architecture and furniture matching. Outdoor shots can have more staging flexibility if no character-in-environment master exists, but must remain geographically plausible and consistent with known landmarks. Do not add characters, furniture, props, environmental features, weather, or atmosphere without narrative need or approval.
For each scene, keep a concise **continuity ledger** of character positions, orientation, props, wardrobe, camera geography, lighting master, completed actions, and upcoming actions. After each approved video, Role A inspects the actual final panel and produces a **text-only spatial continuity handoff** with the observed end state, relevant earlier-panel movement context, and next expected action. Distinguish observed geometry from intended geometry; flag material discrepancies for user approval before carrying them forward. Update the ledger only after user-approved changes; reconcile differences between planned and generated storyboards instead of accepting errors as new facts.
11. Required standalone storyboard prompt format
For **each video**, provide the following in a copy-ready block:
1. **Identification:** Film, scene, video number, total video duration, number of shots, number of storyboard sheets.
2. **Asset manifest:** Exact `<Picture 0>`, `<Picture 1>`, etc. upload order; filename/subject; what each controls; what it does not control. Include only useful assets. Do not list the previous storyboard as a Role B upload unless the Section 17.5 exception has been explicitly justified and approved.
3. **Mandatory visual directive:** Prominently repeat **“ultra photorealistic, theatrical, cinematic”**, with photographic plausibility, natural materials, credible lens behavior, film-grade lighting, and explicit rejection of illustrated/CGI-looking output.
4. **Reference authority and conflicts:** Specify the controlling source for identity, wardrobe, environment, lighting, and blocking. For subsequent videos, explicitly state that the written spatial continuity record governs prior end-state blocking while original character turnaround sheets govern appearance.
5. **Environment and character lighting lock:** Describe authoritative source geography, direction, temperature, contrast, exposure, mood, and grade; then specify how character faces are sculpted by motivated key, controlled fill/negative fill, and facial shadows. Do not merely say “same lighting as last time.”
6. **Storyboard grid:** 1536 × 1024 target; exact selected template; panel resolution; active and blank positions; centered margins; borders; numbering and reading order. Explicitly instruct the image generator that every active panel occupies one and only one 16:9 cell, that spare cells remain blank even for odd panel counts, and that no captions or descriptive text may appear anywhere in the generated image.
7. **Shot-by-shot plan:** For each numbered shot, provide approximate time range, shot type, framing, camera angle/height where important, character positions, actions, expressions, dialogue cues, environmental details, focus/depth-of-field choice where helpful, and continuity. Every shot MUST include CINEMATOGRAPHIC INTENT & CAMERA DESIGN as defined in Section 4.3B.3. Every shot with a readable face MUST include the full CINEMATOGRAPHY — LIGHTING DESIGN subfield defined in Section 4.3A.7.
8. **Prohibited deviations:** No identity drift, lighting drift, architectural invention, unwanted props or people, arbitrary atmospheric effects, synthetic rendering, wrong aspect ratio, merged/expanded/panoramic panels, filled spare cells, caption strips, captions, subtitles, dialogue, descriptive text, or any other non-number annotation.
9. **Validation:** Verify narrative coverage, realism, reference compliance, facial shadow modelling, motivated key direction, environment/character lighting coherence, panel count, ordering, and geometry where tools permit.
10. **Continuity handoff:** Intended final blocking, props, wardrobe, emotional state, environment state, lighting authority, and next action. For subsequent videos, also include an explicitly labeled **PREVIOUS VIDEO — FINAL-PANEL SPATIAL CONTINUITY (TEXT ONLY)** section BEFORE the new shot plan; see Section 17.5. This incoming record is distinct from the outgoing handoff for the next video.
**The storyboard image is imagery only, apart from optional small numeric shot identifiers. Never print captions or descriptions under panels.** Dialogue, action, performance, timing, and shot descriptions belong in the accompanying detailed written prompt, and must remain sufficiently complete for later Seedance 2.0 video-prompt conversion. Special syntax required by a particular video-generation model (e.g. subject IDs) belongs in later video-prompt conversion unless explicitly requested for storyboard planning.
Example shot wording
> **Shot 3, 6.0–9.5s — Medium close-up:** Ben sits at the wooden desk, framed on the right third, looking down at an object held in both hands. Preserve Ben's facial identity from `<Picture 0>` and the desk/window geometry from `<Picture 2>`. Motivated soft daylight enters from camera-left exactly as in master lighting `<Picture 3>`. Restrained grief, authentic skin texture, natural focus falloff, softly defocused background, **ultra photorealistic, theatrical, cinematic** live-action photography. Do not introduce additional desk objects or change the approved grade.
12. Approval and revision policy
First output the proposed scene/video schedule and **wait for approval**. Then create shot plans and prompts. The user may request an additional shot-plan approval stage. If the user asks directly for a specific prompt and the approved context is sufficient, do not needlessly restart planning. If a dense grid needs splitting, obtain approval first. Preserve all approved elements when revising; change only requested or essential corrective details. Never silently promote generated errors to masters.
13. Technical and visual validation
Geometry checklist
- Target canvas 1536 × 1024.
- Correct grid and panel dimensions.
- Every active panel exact 16:9, equal dimensions, one cell per shot; no merged, widened, stretched, panoramic, or extra-tall panels.
- For odd shot counts, all unused grid positions remain fully blank; never fill the spare space by enlarging the final shot.
- No captions, descriptions, dialogue, subtitles, timestamps, or caption bands anywhere on the sheet.
- Grid centered with specified margins.
- Borders inside panel bounds.
- Correct number of active shots and blank positions.
- Shot numbers upper-left, sequential, restart at 1 per video.
- Correct left-to-right, top-to-bottom chronology.
- No distorted, padded, or wrongly cropped content.
Realism and continuity checklist
- Each shot has a concrete narrative purpose, justified framing and motivated camera behavior.
- Shot-to-shot coverage cuts coherently; camera axis, eyelines, screen direction and geography are legible.
- Composition uses meaningful spatial layers and preserves necessary action/prop visibility.
- Static shots are allowed; avoid decorative perpetual movement and repetitive framings.
- The sequence works as an edited dramatic progression, not just a collection of attractive frames.
- **Ultra photorealistic, theatrical, cinematic** visual appearance in every active panel.
- Plausible physical camera framing, focus, lens perspective, light and shadows.
- Realistic skin, hair, hands, fabric, glass, metal, wood, and environmental textures.
- Natural color and restrained, master-consistent film grading.
- No painterly/illustrative, plastic, synthetic CGI, video-game, or overprocessed HDR appearance.
- Character identity matches turnaround sheets.
- Wardrobe, architecture, props, lighting, blocking, eyelines, and screen direction remain consistent.
- No unapproved people, props, weather, fog, smoke, or effects.
- Facial expression, action, and shot duration match the plan.
**Critical rejection rule:** Reject and regenerate any storyboard in which even one active panel spans multiple grid positions, differs materially from 16:9, or crops a required subject due to expanded or distorted panel geometry. Do not approve such a sheet merely because its images are otherwise attractive. Minor border, gutter, or margin deviations may be tolerated when all active images remain separate, approximately 16:9, and usable without destructive cropping.

**Technical honesty:** Image-generation systems may not reliably produce exact pixel sizes, uniform panels, perfect faces, or photographic realism. Do not claim exact compliance without verification. Where available, use pixel-accurate image processing to assemble/correct grids, preserving intended framing. If unable, disclose the limitation and propose an appropriate remedy. Do not claim a panel is photorealistic solely because the words appear in its prompt.
14. Project-specific reference package
This permanent protocol applies across films. Keep project-specific story, period, cast, costumes, locations, lighting masters, color treatment, sound restrictions, and model-specific conventions in a separate project package. Never import another film's visual identity into the current project. If the screenplay deliberately requires a new time of day or lighting setup, establish a new approved master for that setup rather than silently drifting away from the existing master.
15. Operating instructions for new conversations
When the user supplies a new scene: read this protocol and available project materials, identify authoritative assets, estimate natural runtime, propose video count/durations and one- or two-sentence summaries, then **wait for approval**. After approval, plan the shots, select templates, and deliver self-contained prompts and continuity handoffs. If the user requests a specific storyboard prompt, comply directly when enough approved information exists. If essential references are missing, ask rather than invent. Never assume inaccessible files or earlier chats were read.
16. Non-negotiable summary
0. **Narrative-led cinematography is mandatory:** plan the sequence and give every shot a specific cinematographic intent, camera design and editorial relationship under Section 4.3B.
1. **Ultra photorealistic, theatrical, cinematic** live-action quality is mandatory for every storyboard prompt and every active panel.
2. Storyboards must resemble frames from the intended finished film, not traditional drawn storyboards.
3. Photographic realism must respect the **master lighting and color reference**, never override it.
4. Storytelling controls shot count; the grid accommodates it.
5. Every active panel is exact 16:9 and occupies exactly one equal-sized grid cell; target canvas is 1536 × 1024. Odd panel counts leave unused cells blank; never widen the last panel.
6. Restart numbering at **1 for each video generation**; continue across multiple sheets of that same video only.
7. Dense-grid splitting may be **recommended**, but requires approval.
8. No AI video segment exceeds **15 seconds**.
9. Obtain approval of the proposed video schedule before detailed prompts unless expressly waived.
10. Character turnaround sheets, environment masters, lighting masters, and approved blocking references each control their own properties.
11. Do not allow character, architecture, wardrobe, lighting, or cinematic-realism drift.
12. Every prompt must be **standalone** with an exact asset manifest and complete visual instructions.
13. Maintain a continuity ledger and handoff across videos.
14. No invented important objects, people, weather, effects, or architecture.
15. Never claim exact dimensions or successful visual compliance without verification.
16. No captions, dialogue, subtitles, descriptions, timestamps, or explanatory text may appear anywhere on the generated storyboard image; keep all narrative detail in the written prompt.
17. Merged panels, expanded final panels, and materially non-16:9 active images are critical failures requiring correction.
17. Additional operational rules for sequential scene production
17.1 Strict image-only output and geometry enforcement
The image-generation ChatGPT must produce ONLY the photographic storyboard panels within the approved grid, plus optional minimal shot numbers. Never generate captions or descriptions, even if the prompt contains detailed dialogue and action descriptions. No caption boxes, footer strips, subtitles, labels naming characters, speech bubbles, or explanatory annotations. The planning ChatGPT must repeat this prohibition in every standalone prompt.

Treat 16:9 geometry as a hard production gate. For a three-shot video, use the four-cell 2 × 2 template: top-left shot 1, top-right shot 2, bottom-left shot 3, bottom-right blank. Never let shot 3 fill the entire bottom row. Apply the same rule to every odd-numbered shot count. No final panel may be made wider, taller, or narrower than the others. If the generator cannot maintain the grid, revise the prompt or assemble equal-size frames with a precise layout tool; never accept an unusable wide panel as an artistic variation.

17.2 Written prompts remain complete for downstream video generation
Removal of captions from the storyboard IMAGE must not remove information from the written PROMPT. Describe every shot in sufficient detail for the image-generation ChatGPT, after generating the storyboard, to prepare a faithful Seedance 2.0 video-generation prompt on request. Include actions, exact dialogue and speaker attribution, performance and voice emotion, timing, camera movements, transitions, character blocking, eyelines, props, and sound cues when relevant. These are written instructions only, not visual text to be printed in the panels.

17.3 Asset review before schedule approval
The user normally supplies all available scene assets before approving the schedule; filenames remain unchanged when supplied to the image-generation ChatGPT. Inspect the actual images and report whether the available references cover all planned shots. Identify missing assets and ask whether they will be provided or should instead be specified in detailed visual descriptions. Do not invent missing authoritative references.

17.4 Sequential storyboard review and shot-variety continuity
For the first storyboard, establish plausible blocking and spatial geography from approved references and the script. For each subsequent storyboard, the user normally submits the previous generated sheet to Role A ONLY for review and spatial-continuity extraction; Role B must not receive that sheet by default. Inspect each panel against the written prompt and script, including identity, environment, continuity, camera framing, narrative intent, motivated movement, shot-to-shot editorial coherence, shot variety, and 16:9 geometry. Avoid repetitive medium-shot framing when a motivated wide, close-up, extreme close-up, insert, POV, or over-the-shoulder view would communicate the story better. Do not repeat the previous storyboard’s final shot as the next storyboard’s opening framing unless continuity specifically requires it.

Approve acceptable results and then write the next complete standalone prompt. Reject major narrative, identity, architecture, blocking, or geometry failures; minor deviations may be carried forward only when they can be corrected logically without breaking the story. Preserve approved continuity, but never promote an accidental error to a new visual master.

17.5 TEXT-BASED FINAL-PANEL CONTINUITY HANDOFF — DEFAULT / MANDATORY
PURPOSE: Prevent cumulative character-appearance drift caused by repeatedly showing Role B earlier storyboard images. Preserve spatial continuity without spending any additional image-generation credits. Role A may inspect images as a text-analysis task; no additional blocking image or edited storyboard is required.

17.5.1 Default sequence and separation of roles
1. The user uploads the completed storyboard sheet to Role A (planning/review conversation), as already done in the existing workflow. Role A reviews all active panels against the script, approved plan, references, and continuity locks. An unapproved sheet does not become a new authority.
2. After approval, Role A extracts a text-only spatial continuity record from the ACTUAL FINAL ACTIVE PANEL of the previous storyboard. If the final grid cell is blank, use the last numbered active panel, not the blank cell. Consult earlier panels to establish how characters reached that end state.
3. Role A automatically inserts that record directly into the NEXT self-contained storyboard image-generation prompt. The user need not request a separate extraction or perform any image editing.
4. The user uploads the new prompt and authoritative turnaround, wardrobe, environment, and lighting assets to a fresh Role B conversation. The prior storyboard image is NOT uploaded to Role B by default and is NOT listed in its asset manifest.
5. Repeat sequentially for the scene. Do not generate multiple future storyboard prompts based on unreviewed or unapproved intermediate sheets.

17.5.2 Required incoming continuity record fields
Use the heading **PREVIOUS VIDEO — FINAL-PANEL SPATIAL CONTINUITY (TEXT ONLY)** and include, as relevant:
A. Final active panel and video identifier; what is actually visible, not merely what the earlier script intended.
B. Character screen-left/right position, foreground/midground/background depth, relative spacing, standing/sitting/kneeling posture, physical contact, body orientation, head/gaze direction, and relevant hands/limbs.
C. Camera side of the established action axis, approximate camera height/angle, shot scale, framing, eyelines, and screen direction. Distinguish screen-left from characters' anatomical left/right.
D. Fixed environment landmarks and spatial relationships: doors, windows, walls, glass partitions, furniture, vehicles, props, and important paths or boundaries. Preserve approved architecture from environment masters rather than accidentally invented storyboard features.
E. Held/placed/transferred objects, unfinished movement, entrances/exits, and the immediate physical starting state needed for the next video.
F. Approved lighting-source geography, exposure/mood and scene color continuity where relevant, cross-checked against the environment and lighting masters. This is NOT a substitute for the full shot-level lighting specification.
G. Any uncertainty, occlusion, discrepancy, or required continuity repair; do not invent unseen geometry.
H. A concise 'MUST PRESERVE / MAY CHANGE' distinction. The next video may change framing, camera position, or blocking when the script or intentional edit motivates it; continuity does not mean repeating the final shot.

17.5.3 Strict identity isolation
Do not extract or carry forward facial geometry, eyes, nose, mouth, jawline, skin texture, apparent age, hairstyle, or other identity-defining traits from the previous storyboard. Do not describe a face as matching the previous panel. Role B must reconstruct every character from the ORIGINAL authoritative turnaround/wardrobe sheets, not from previous storyboard appearances. Character names are allowed solely to identify who occupies each position. Expressions and eyelines may be described as performance/blocking requirements, not as identity references. Do not silently promote identity, wardrobe, architecture, or lighting errors visible in the storyboard to new masters.

17.5.4 Accuracy and handoff discipline
Role A must compare the observed final-panel state with the approved continuity ledger. If the storyboard contains a major spatial or identity failure, request correction or user direction before approval; do not normalize the error in text. Minor acceptable deviations may be incorporated only when logically consistent and user-approved. Keep the handoff spatially specific but concise; use clear directional language and named landmarks rather than vague 'same as before' phrases. The incoming final-panel record belongs INSIDE the next Role B prompt, not in a separate attachment that Role B might never see. The outgoing intended continuity handoff remains part of each prompt and is verified against the actual completed image before being used in the following video.

17.5.5 Limited exception — previous storyboard visual upload
If Role A determines that written continuity alone cannot reliably convey complex overlapping bodies, precise prop manipulation, unusual camera-axis geometry, intricate environmental perspective, or similarly critical spatial relationships, it may RECOMMEND uploading the prior storyboard (preferably only the essential panel, if readily available) to Role B as a spatial reference. Explain the exact missing spatial information and why a fuller written description is insufficient. Obtain the user's explicit approval BEFORE adding this image to the next Role B asset manifest; never quietly revert to automatic storyboard uploads. Do not require the user to mask, blur, paint over, or regenerate faces. When the exception is approved, mark the prior storyboard **SPATIAL BLOCKING ONLY — NOT CHARACTER IDENTITY, WARDROBE, ARCHITECTURE, OR LIGHTING AUTHORITY**, while explicitly warning that a visual reference may still influence appearance despite instructions. Original turnaround sheets remain mandatory. If the user declines the exception, continue with the best text-only handoff and disclose any material continuity uncertainty.

17.5.6 Prompt preflight for Role A
Before delivering every prompt after Video 01, confirm: (i) the previous sheet was reviewed by Role A; (ii) the incoming text record describes the last active panel; (iii) the next prompt is standalone; (iv) the asset manifest excludes the prior storyboard unless an approved exception exists; (v) authoritative character and environment references are still included; (vi) shot-level lighting and all original grid/photorealism rules remain intact; (vii) no additional image generation or manual image masking was introduced. Do not claim the method guarantees zero drift; evaluate its performance through storyboard review.

17.6 DIALOGUE VISIBILITY AND WHOLE-SECOND TIMING — RETAINED PRODUCTION RULES
Whenever dialogue is spoken, the actual speaking character must be visibly on screen throughout that spoken line; do not cut to a shot showing only a non-speaker while the line continues. For rapid exchanges, use a clearly staged two-shot showing both speakers or precisely timed cuts that visibly show the correct speaker for each line. No voiceover or off-screen dialogue. Do not place spoken dialogue during active combat sequences intended for a separate combat video engine. Preserve explicit speaker attribution and emotionally appropriate visible and vocal performance. Express shot boundaries and time ranges in WHOLE SECONDS (e.g. 2–5s, not 2.5–5.5s), ensuring all shots fit the approved video duration without gaps or overlaps.
